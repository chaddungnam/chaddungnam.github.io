Designing a Game with Vibe Coding, Why I Majored in Industrial Design Yet Didn't Make a Single Asset Myself | Quirky Ball

I majored in industrial design, but

I did not make the game assets for Quirky Ball myself.

 

That means I did not make them by drawing illustrations one by one,

exporting buttons in Photoshop,

and refining icons manually

in that fashion.

 

Yet the atmosphere of the game is gradually coming together into a cohesive direction.

 

At first, I found this a bit strange myself.

 

I graduated from design school, yet I wasn't drawing my own game art.

 

However, to finish the game to the end,

this approach was actually the right one.

 

Graduating from design school doesn't mean you have to draw every piece of art yourself.

When you study industrial design,

you often appear to be someone who is good at drawing.

 

However, the design I learned

was closer to sorting out problems

than making pretty pictures.

 

It is about deciding

who is looking at this screen,

what they need to know first,

and what actions to guide them toward naturally.

 

My role at Quirky Ball was the same.

 

Instead of drawing marbles myself,

I determined what shape would make them distinguishable at a glance.

 

Instead of pixel-crafting scientists myself,

I judged how well this character conveyed

the quirky laboratory vibe of the game.

 

Not drawing pictures did not mean I wasn't doing design.

 

If you start by making assets, the game will never end.

In 1 development, the scariest thing is not failing to make something.

 

Things to make keep piling up,

while the game remains in an unplayable state.

 

Even if you are making just one home screen,

you need a background, logo, buttons, icons,

badges, pop-ups, and characters.

 

If I had tried to finish all of this by hand

before starting development,

I would still be picking colors

on the first screen.

 

So I changed the order.

 

I first created a screen that was playable,

and fixed awkward parts within the actual game.

 

I pulled necessary visual elements quickly using AI and code,

and grouped only the surviving ones into design rules.

 

Since I didn't wait for finished assets,

I could compare screens much faster.

 

Most of Quirky Ball is drawn with rules rather than images.

If you look inside the project, unexpectedly,

there are far more screens drawn with code than image files.

 

Marble faces and highlights,

card borders and shadows,

button presses, small badges, and icons

are combined inside the game.

 

The advantage of this method

is that fixing one thing improves multiple screens at once.

 

If you establish rules for

rounded corner sizes, shadow directions,

and accent colors,

you don't need to create new images for every shop card.

 

Even if the contents differ, like packages, currencies, tickets, and cosmetics,

they end up looking

like screens from the same game.

 

Things weren't organized this way from the start.

 

Every button had a different color,

icon expression methods were mixed,

and the order of information varied across cards.

 

Instead of fixing each one to look pretty,

fixing the common rules first

began organizing the entire screen rapidly.

 

The design I did was about choosing and discarding.

When you do vibe coding, results come out very quickly.

 

The problem is that not only good results come out fast.

 

Screens that look plausible but don't fit the game,

icons that stick out awkwardly,

and hard-to-read color combinations also pour out together.

 

So the task I did most was

It was not about creating something new

but about comparing and discarding things.

 

I captured execution screens,

checked what was seen first,

and wrote down the problem in a single sentence.

 

I looked at the modified screen again,

and if it still felt awkward, I discarded it once more.

 

The ranking screen went through the same process.

 

Instead of simply listing ranking numbers,

the podium, my rank, season information,

and entrances to various rankings

were organized to be read on a single screen.

 

Here, design was not decoration, but determining the order of information.

 

A button that is not accidentally pressed came before a pretty button.

Simply making the game screen look pretty

does not raise the level of completion.

 

How the player's fingers actually move

must also be designed.

 

In Quirky Ball, when your level increases,

a screen appears where you choose one of two growth directions.

screen appears where you choose.

 

However, if the fingers that were tapping the screen rapidly

in the previous action remain as they are,

as soon as the pop-up appears,

you might end up pressing an unwanted choice.

 

Therefore, the selection button appears dimmed for the first 1.5 seconds

and does not accept input.

 

As time passes, its color and contrast return

and the button becomes active.

 

The dimmed state and the vivid state must be clearly different

so that players can immediately understand

whether it can be pressed right now.

 

Things like this are easily missed

in the task of making just one screenshot look pretty.

 

However, if you continuously run and build the actual game,

you will eventually discover them.

 

The completion level of game design as I think of it

comes from such small safety devices.

 

Design is not automatically completed just because AI made it.

AI can create screens quickly.

 

However, it does not know what kind of screen feels like Quirky Ball.

 

Which button should be shown larger,

which joke should be kept and which decoration should be discarded,

and whether text is actually legible on a mobile screen

must ultimately be decided by a human.

 

What I did

was not telling the AI to make something look cool once

and then finishing there.

 

I repeated running the game,

capturing it,

describing the problem specifically,

and comparing it again.

 

Ultimately, in vibe coding,

the moment when a sense of design is needed

was the moment of choosing rather than the moment of creating.

 

If I were to make it again, would I do the same?

I would probably do the same.

 

Instead of making a perfect asset bundle from the start,

I would start by creating playable screens.

 

And when shapes and colors used repeatedly

begin to appear,

I would bind them into design rules.

 

Instead of drawing directly,

I will look more,

speak more accurately,

and discard more coldly.

 

The reason why I, a design graduate,

did not create a single asset myself

is not because I gave up on drawing.

 

It is because I chose a design method

that allows me to complete a game all the way to the end even by myself.
