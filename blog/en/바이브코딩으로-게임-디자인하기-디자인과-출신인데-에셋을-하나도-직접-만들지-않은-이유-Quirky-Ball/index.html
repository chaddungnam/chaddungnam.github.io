Designing a Game with Vibe Coding: Why a Design Graduate Didn't Make a Single Asset Directly | Quirky Ball

I majored in industrial design, but

I did not directly make the game assets for Quirky Ball.

 

This means I did not make them by drawing illustrations one by one,

exporting buttons from Photoshop,

and directly refining icons

in that manner.

 

Even so, the atmosphere of the game is gradually converging into one.

 

At first, I found this a bit strange too.

 

Graduating from design school, yet not actually drawing my own game art.

 

However, to finish the game to the end,

this method was actually the right one.

 

Just because you graduated from design school doesn't mean you have to draw every piece of art yourself.

Studying industrial design

often makes people think you are good at drawing.

 

However, the design I learned

was closer to organizing problems

than to making pretty pictures.

 

It is the work of deciding

who is looking at this screen,

what they need to know first,

and what actions to naturally induce.

 

At Quirky Ball as well, my role was the same.

 

Instead of drawing the marbles directly,

I decided what shape they needed to be to be distinguished at a glance.

 

Instead of pixel-crafting the scientist directly,

I judged how well this character conveyed

the quirky laboratory vibe of the game.

 

Not drawing pictures did not mean not doing design.

 

If you start by making assets, the game is never finished.

In 1 development, the scariest thing is not failing to make things.

 

The things to make keep increasing,

while the game still remains in a state where it cannot be executed.

 

Even if you are just making a single home screen,

you need a background, a logo, buttons, icons,

badges, pop-ups, and characters.

 

If I had tried to complete all of this by hand

before starting development,

I would still be picking colors

on the first screen.

 

So I changed the order.

 

I first created a screen that could be played,

and fixed awkward parts within the actual game.

 

I quickly pulled out the necessary visual elements using AI and code,

and grouped only the ones that survived into design rules.

 

Since I wasn't waiting for finished assets,

I could compare screens much faster.

 

Most of Quirky Ball is drawn with rules rather than images.

If you look closely at the project, surprisingly,

there are far more screens drawn with code than with image files.

 

The marble's face and highlights,

the card's border and shadow,

the pressing feel of buttons, and small badges and icons

are combined inside the game.

 

The advantage of this method is

that fixing one thing improves multiple screens at the same time.

 

If you establish rules for

the size of rounded corners,

the direction of shadows,

and accent colors,

 

you do not need to create new images for every shop card.

Whether the content is different, such as packages, currency, tickets, or cosmetics,

they end up looking like screens from the same game.

 

It wasn't organized like this from the beginning.

 

Every button had a different color,

the expression methods of icons were mixed,

and the order of information varied across cards.

 

Instead of fixing each one prettily,

fixing the common rules first

began to quickly organize the entire screen.

 

The design I did was about choosing and discarding.

When you do vibe coding, results come out very quickly.

 

The problem is that it's not just good results that come out quickly.

 

Screens that look plausible but don't fit the game,

icons that stick out alone,

and hard-to-read color combinations are poured out together.

 

So the work I did the most was

It was not about creating something new,

but about comparing and discarding things.

 

I captured the execution screens,

checked what was visible first,

and wrote down the problem in a single sentence.

 

I looked at the modified screen again,

and if it still felt awkward, I discarded it again.

 

The ranking screen went through the same process.

 

Instead of just listing ranking numbers,

I organized the podium, my rank, season information,

and entrances to various rankings

so they could be read on a single screen.

 

Here, design was not decoration, but the act of determining the order of information.

 

A button that is not accidentally pressed came before a pretty button.

Simply making the game screens look pretty

does not increase completeness.

 

How the player's fingers actually move

must also be designed.

 

In Quirky Ball, when your level increases,

a screen appears where you choose

one of two growth directions.

 

However, if the finger that was rapidly tapping the screen

in the previous action remains there,

as soon as the pop-up appears,

you might end up pressing an unintended choice.

 

Therefore, the selection button is dimmed and accepts no input for the first 1.5 seconds.

dimmed and accepts no input.

 

As time passes, color and contrast return

and the button becomes active.

 

The dimmed state and the vivid state must be distinctly different

so the player immediately understands

whether it can be pressed right now.

 

Things like this are easily missed

when working on making just a single screenshot look pretty.

 

However, if you build while continuously running the actual game,

you eventually discover them.

 

The completeness of game design as I see it

comes from such small safety devices.

 

Design is not automatically completed just because AI made it.

AI can make screens quickly.

 

However, it does not know what kind of screen is truly like Quirky Ball.

 

Which button should be shown larger,

which joke should be kept and which decoration should be discarded,

and whether text is actually readable on a mobile screen

must ultimately be decided by a person.

 

What I did

was not telling the AI to make something look cool once

and calling it done.

 

I repeated the process of running the game,

capturing it,

stating the problem specifically,

and comparing again.

 

Ultimately, in vibe coding,

the moment that requires a sense of design

was the moment of choosing, rather than the moment of creating.

 

If I made it again, would I do it the same way?

I would probably do it the same way.

 

Instead of creating a perfect asset bundle from the start,

I would start by creating playable screens.

 

And when shapes and colors used repeatedly

start to become visible,

I would bundle them into design rules.

 

Instead of drawing directly,

I will look more,

speak more precisely,

and discard more ruthlessly.

 

The reason I, a design major,

did not create a single asset myself

is not because I gave up on drawing.

 

It is because I chose a design method

that allows me to finish a game all the way to the end even on my own.
