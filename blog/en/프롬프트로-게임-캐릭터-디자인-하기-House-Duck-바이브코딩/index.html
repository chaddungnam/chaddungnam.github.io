When you ask AI to create a game character,

it produces a plausible-looking image quite quickly.

 

The problem was what came next.

 

When asked to redraw the same person, the face changed,

changing the expression also changed the clothes,

and when scaled down into the game screen, the eyes and mouth became a single lump.

Looking at just one image, you like it,

but when you put multiple images side by side, many times they did not look like the same game.

 

Pulling out pretty images and

creating game characters were different tasks.

 

Even if you get a drawing you like with a single prompt,

whether that drawing survives inside the game was a separate issue.

 

Project K, Quirky Ball,

and while making a next project

whose title had not even been decided yet, I learned.

 

A single well-drawn picture was the most dangerous.

The early character art of Project K

looks very clearly like generative imagery when viewed now.

 

 

Just looking at one picture, the character seemed to have presence.

 

However, wanting to show the characteristics of the world view quickly,

I repeatedly inserted similar appearance conditions,

so the leader candidates all gathered with similar ages and body types.

 

 

Expressions became more varied,

but if you covered the names, it was hard to immediately tell who was who.

 

So instead of increasing the number of figures,

I changed to first widening

the face shape, body type, hairstyle, and clothing silhouette.

 

 

It passed automated checks a lot

and the image size and file specifications matched.

 

And yet, when put into an actual mobile screen,

fine details like hair, clothing wrinkles, and gloss remained,

giving a feeling of clashing with the pixel screen of the game.

 

 

Just because the file was normal did not mean the artwork was good.

 

Eventually, Project K abandoned the direction of applying a heavier pixel filter

and retook the direction of first properly making a single 3D original

and then extracting portraits, profiles, and card images from that original.

 

 

The image above is also not the final game asset.

It is closer to the currently approved character direction board,

and the actual 3D original to be put into the game is still being made.

 

Finding a direction you liked

and not talking as if it were finished was also quite important.

 

I changed prompts from being appreciation reviews to production instructions.

At first, I thought that putting in lots of adjectives like

cool, high-end, or cinematic would make it better.

 

Now I write the opposite.

 

I first write down what role this character has,

what size they appear on screen,

and what must not change.

 

Role: A leader immediately readable on mobile screens

Distinction: Face shape, body type, hair, long coat silhouette

Fixed: Front, side, and back views of the same character, same outfit and signature color

Shading: 2 to 3 step cel shading centered on base colors and shadows

Forbidden: Real person lookalikes, skin noise, micro wrinkles, text and watermarks

Inspection: Even when scaled down to actual game size, eyes, nose, mouth and role should be readable

 

This is not the exact original prompt used at the time.

It is a reorganization of the production conditions left behind

after failing multiple times, formatted into prompt form.

 

And we do not make dozens of them at once.

We pull out only a few representative characters first,

put them into the actual UI and game screen, and then move on to the next batch.

 

It was faster to establish rejection criteria first than to increase generation attempts.

 

Quirky Ball became a character by drawing less, ironically.

Quirky Ball did not have its current face from the beginning either.

 

At one point, we even tried attaching faces to regular beads

The red hexagonal Quorky was wearing a big-toothed smile.

 

When everything on the screen looked like a character,

it actually became blurry who the main character was.

 

So, we removed the faces from the ordinary marbles

and left only Quorky as a character with four-white eyes and thick eyebrows,

and the corners of its mouth raised on one side.

 

 

Fixing the facial expressions alone was not enough.

 

Telling its name, explaining why it was trapped in the lab,

and having it blink, smile, worry, and provoke depending on the gameplay situation

was only when the red shape began to be read as a character.

 

Cutscenes went through a similar process.

Initially, scientists and machines were drawn as simple shapes.

 

Later, we obtained drafts of scientists and equipment using generative images,

but we did not put those results directly into the game.

 

We reduced the colors to five, erased the white background,

and fixed the size and pixel scale to bundle them back into a single atlas.

 

Only after cutting them to the same specification

did the scientist's face and machine size not shake even when the scene changed.

 

Post-processing rules survived longer than good prompts.

 

In the upcoming untitled game, we deliberately did not make the final characters.

The early prototype of the upcoming game

was close to a timing game without even characters.

 

During the reboot, we added ducks, geese, and pigeons,

but the birds visible now are low-poly greyboxes made with code.

They are not the final character designs.

 

Instead, what we decided first were the roles.

 

Ducks were divided into command and balance,

geese into defense and weight,

and pigeons into characters that create paths.

 

Even if the models are not pretty,

we first see if dragging, collisions, cover, and rewards are read properly.

 

 

It has not yet passed fun verification by external players either.

 

So, instead of making a bunch of final cell 3D characters first,

we stopped with the plan to review only the original of a single duck species and expand later.

 

Because adding ten more pretty characters to an unfun game

does not make it a fun game.

 

Just because you like the assets does not mean the game is finished.

When character images turn out well,

it suddenly feels like the project is mostly completed.

 

Every time I put a new bird drawing on the screen, I too

deluded myself into thinking the game had become quite plausible.

 

However, whether players will run it again because it is fun,

whether the software is completed enough that saving, logging in, and payments are not broken, whether the software is completed,

whether the UI is clear enough that even a first-time viewer can understand it, whether the UI is clear,

whether the interaction is nimble enough to respond instantly when a button is pressed, whether the interaction is nimble,

and whether it extends to marketing to announce the existence of the game

cannot be solved by a single character concept art.

 

Rather, the better the assets get,

the higher the standards players expect from the game rise as well.

 

Assets make the starting line look nice.

But ultimately what drives a game is

fun and completeness.

 

If a prompt works well and a character you like comes out,

you can be happy.

 

However, that just means the project went well for that day,

not that the game has succeeded.

 

The next day, you must play again, fix things,

and check the response of a single button and the fun of the first 10 minutes.

 

There is still too much left to complete the game to be overly swayed by every little thing.
