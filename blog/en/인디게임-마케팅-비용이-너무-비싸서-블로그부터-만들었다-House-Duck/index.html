Indie Game Marketing Costs Were Too High So I Started With a Blog | House Duck

When I finished making the game,

I thought I just had to show it to people now.

 

However, while looking into indie game marketing costs,

I quietly closed the browser window.

 

Whether the game is fun and whether people will keep playing it

haven't been verified with numbers yet,

and I wasn't in a situation to run large-scale ads.

 

So I decided to spend time instead of money.

 

Rather than a game made by an unknown creator,

a game showing someone making mistakes, fixing them, and trying again

felt like it could be remembered a little longer.

 

Rather than a game that appears briefly through ads and vanishes,

I wanted to become a game that leaves behind the creator's worries.

That is how I decided to use the House Duck blog

as the starting point for marketing.

 

While trying to promote the game, I ended up making the company website first.

The first step was creating the address for House Duck.

 

Porkbun at houseduck.in created the domain

and connected the company website and blog to that address.

 

I didn't want to leave the site sitting around like a mere business card.

 

What kind of game is currently being made,

who operates it, and where to contact if a problem occurs

were made so that even a first-time visitor could easily find them.

 

Although I received a lot of help from AI in building the site,

the colors, sentences, screen order, and mobile appearance

were continually opened and revised by me.

 

Even if someone who clicked an ad doesn't install it right away,

for someone who stumbled upon it while searching,

"They are truly making a game here."

I wanted them to feel at least that much.

 

Once I made a site, documents kept following.

After creating the homepage,

terms of use, privacy policy, and Impressum were waiting.

 

I am doing business in Germany, not Korea,

and the game can also be met by EU users.

 

Therefore, sentences from a Korean site

could not just be copied and pasted to finish it.

 

EU privacy guide and

German DDG business information display standards were checked while

writing down what information is handled and why, how long it is stored,

and where to contact when deletion is desired.

 

I couldn't just leave it out because I didn't know.

 

The currently published documents

have GDPR-related items prepared, taking EU users into consideration.

 

This does not mean it is a perfect answer with all legal reviews completed.

If the features used in the game and site change,

the documents must also be reviewed and fixed accordingly.

 

I cannot say it is prepared perfectly.

Still, I wanted to write down in advance what users provide, where to contact,

and what they can ask to be deleted.

Creating two company emails was also a task.

If a problem occurs with the game

and players have nowhere to contact, it becomes troublesome.

 

Instead of exposing a personal Gmail address,

at ImprovMX support@houseduck.in and

business@houseduck.in were created.

 

I separated inquiries and business addresses

and connected incoming mail so it can be checked in familiar Gmail

and replied to using the company address.

 

I went a step further from here.

 

Instead of constantly rummaging through the inbox,

I built a feature in the House Duck admin console to search inquiries,

categorize processing status, and handle reply templates and attachments

all on a single screen.

 

The goal is not to imitate a grandiose customer center.

When an inquiry comes in later,

even if I am not in front of my laptop, I can check it on my mobile phone

and not miss urgent issues.

 

Rather than preparing after the user base grows,

I made it beforehand so as not to miss a single visitor when one arrives.

 

If this is an era read by AI, AI also needed an entrance.

These days, people enter by typing directly into the search bar, but

there have also been many cases of asking AI about games or companies.

 

Therefore, not only sitemaps and robots.txt

but also llms.txt, which AI can reference when reading public information, and

House Duck public MCP were also created.

 

The name might sound a bit difficult, but

it is closer to a guidebook made so that AI can read without wandering much about what kind of company House Duck is and what games it makes.

It does not open up administrator features or personal information,

 

but only allows reading information already published on the site and blog.

Of course, just making an MCP

 

does not suddenly raise Google search rankings.

It is simply paving one more path for both people and AI

 

when someone searches for House Duck.

none

 

Being a lacking 1-person company, I wanted to be more visible.

Even if domains and sites, terms and privacy policies,

and email and customer support screens are built,

game installs do not increase by themselves.

 

Not spending on advertising

does not mean the probability of success increases.

 

Still, to someone who searched and came in,

I did not want to look like an abandoned game and an unreachable company.

 

House Duck is still a lacking 1-person company.

That is why I want to explain more often, receive contacts more easily,

and show the process of fixing problems when they arise.

 

I do not know if this blog will generate sales right away.

 

Still, when someone visits Quirky Ball,

“At least they are operating.”

I hope they can feel at least that much.
