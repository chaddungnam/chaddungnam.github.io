Making Games with Vibe Coding, Why I Chose Godot Instead of Unity | Quirky Ball

Godot was not chosen because it is unconditionally a better engine than Unity.

 

On the MacBook I have,

to make a game alone with AI and keep modifying it,

Godot was the most realistic option.

 

I am not a developer.

I majored in industrial design,

and worked as a game QA for about 3 years.

 

And now, in Germany,

I am making a mobile puzzle game called Quirky Ball.

 

I have also tried Unity and Unreal. But they were heavy.

When I decided to make a game

it is not that I did not know Unity or Unreal.

 

I have touched both before.

 

There is plenty of information,

countless completed games,

and I do not even need to prove that they are good engines.

 

The problem was my situation.

 

The equipment I have is an M2 MacBook Air.

 

It was not a high-performance desktop,

nor was I making a large 3D game as a team.

 

Requesting modifications from the AI,

running it right in the engine,

and fixing it again if something went wrong had to be repeated continuously every day.

 

However, Unity and Unreal

felt quite heavy during these short iterations that I wanted.

If even turning on the engine feels burdensome,

you start feeling like you are serving the engine

rather than making a game.

Godot suited my MacBook and workflow well.

The first thought I had when I opened Godot for the first time was

that there was surprisingly nothing much to it.

 

This sounds like a drawback,

but to me, it was rather an advantage.

 

Opening the project,

modifying it,

running it,

and checking the results was a fast workflow.

 

Quirky Ball is not a massive 3D open-world game.

 

It is a vertical-screen mobile puzzle game

where marbles are dropped and collision movements and UI are continuously refined,

making it a 2D mobile puzzle game.

 

Therefore, rather than massive features I would not even use,

being able to quickly check the screen I just modified was more important.

 

The official Godot documentation also specifies macOS Apple Silicon as a supported target.

In fact, there was no major inconvenience in working back and forth between the editor and the game on the M2 MacBook Air.

 

Reference: https://docs.godotengine.org/en/4.7/about/system_requirements.html

 

Why vibe coding and Godot went well together

The vibe coding I am talking about

is not the method of telling AI "make me a game" and ending it there.

 

When Codex writes the code,

I run the game myself.

 

I check if the buttons are weird,

if the marble movement is not fun,

if the text is cut off,

and if a first-time player can understand the rules.

 

And then I have it modified specifically again.

 

If the AI took on a lot of the developer role,

I essentially played the role of the planner, designer,

and QA.

 

What was important in this process

was not getting perfect code all at once.

 

It was the speed of making small modifications,

running it quickly,

and verifying it with my eyes.

 

Godot made those iterations less exhausting.

The fact that it is free is also hard to ignore for a 1 developer.

Godot is a free open-source engine under the MIT license.

 

It can be used commercially,

and the content and rights of the game I made remain with me.

 

Reference: https://godotengine.org/license/

 

Of course, just because something is free does not automatically mean it is good.

 

However, for an indie developer like 1 who has no revenue yet,

not having to worry about engine costs or policy changes first

was definitely a huge relief.

 

Because that money and mental energy

could be spent on fixing the game even one more time.

That does not mean Godot was convenient in every single way.

If I only write up to this point,

I might look like some kind of Godot evangelist.

 

However, when actually trying to launch a mobile game,

there were definitely parts that were inconvenient.

 

Ads,

in-app purchases,

logins,

and native features for Android and iOS

were a completely different challenge compared to just making a single game screen.

 

Matching plugin versions,

checking each platform separately,

and repeating the process of tracking down issues that did not appear inside the engine but occurred on actual devices became a routine.

 

Compared to an engine with a much larger user base like Unity,

there were moments when it was difficult to find materials that directly matched when hitting a roadblock.

 

Just because AI can write code on your behalf

did not mean these kinds of problems magically disappeared either.

 

Rather, as the amount of code I did not understand rapidly increased,

QA involving direct execution and verification became even more important.

Still, if I were to make Quirky Ball again, would I choose Godot?

If it were this game, I think I would choose it again.

 

To be precise,

under the condition of using a single M2 MacBook Air

to quickly build and fix a 2D mobile game alongside AI, then yes.

 

Conversely, for a large-scale 3D game or

a project that requires attaching a bunch of specific commercial SDKs,

I would also compare other engines first.

 

Choosing an engine is not a religion.

 

It is closer to the issue of choosing an engine that I can finish building with,

rather than the issue of choosing a famous engine.

 

For me, that answer was Godot.

And right now,

rather than the fact that I chose an engine,

actually launching Quirky Ball is much more important.

 

Frequently Asked Questions

- Can you make games with Godot without knowing how to code?

The barrier to entry has lowered significantly with the help of AI.

However, that does not mean you do not have to look at code at all.

The ability to explain what is wrong

and directly execute it to judge the results is still required.

 

- Is Godot the best for vibe coding?

It depends on the project.

I chose Godot because of lightweight 2D mobile games and rapid iteration.

If the asset ecosystem or specific SDKs are more important to you, Unity might be more convenient.

 

- Is game development with Godot possible on an M2 MacBook Air?

2D mobile games like Quirky Ball could be worked on sufficiently.

However, this is experience based on my own project.

With heavy 3D graphics and a larger build scale, the results could vary.
