Making Games with Vibe Coding, Why I Chose Godot Instead of Unity | Quirky Ball

Godot is not chosen because it is unconditionally better than Unity.

 

On the MacBook I have,

to make a game with AI alone and keep modifying it,

Godot was the most realistic choice.

 

I am not a developer.

I majored in industrial design,

and worked as a game QA for about 3 years.

 

Now, living in Germany,

I am making a mobile puzzle game called Quirky Ball.

 

I have also used Unity and Unreal. But they were heavy.

When I decided to make a game,

it is not that I did not know Unity or Unreal.

 

I have touched both in the past.

 

There is plenty of information,

countless finished games,

and I do not even need to prove that they are good engines.

 

The problem was my situation.

 

The equipment I have is an M2 MacBook Air.

 

It was not a high-performance desktop,

nor was I making a large 3D game as a team unit.

 

I had to ask AI for modifications,

run them immediately in the engine,

and fix them again if something looked wrong, repeating this continuously throughout the day.

 

However, Unity and Unreal

felt quite heavy during this short iteration cycle that I wanted.

If just turning on the engine feels burdensome,

you start to feel like you are serving the engine

rather than making a game.

Godot fit well with my MacBook and workflow.

The first thought that came to mind when I first opened Godot was

that there was surprisingly not much to it.

 

This sounds like a disadvantage,

but to me, it was an advantage.

 

Opening the project,

modifying,

running,

and checking the results flowed quickly.

 

Quirky Ball is not a giant 3D open world game.

 

It is a mobile puzzle game

where marbles are dropped on a vertical screen,

and collision movements and UI are continuously refined as a 2D mobile puzzle game.

 

Therefore, rather than massive features I would not even use,

it was more important to quickly check the screen I just modified.

 

The official Godot documentation also specifies Apple Silicon on macOS as a supported target.

Indeed, there was no major inconvenience in working back and forth between the editor and the game on the M2 MacBook Air.

 

Reference: https://docs.godotengine.org/en/4.7/about/system_requirements.html

 

Why vibe coding and Godot went well together

The vibe coding I am talking about

is not a method of telling AI "make me a game" and ending it there.

 

When Codex writes code,

I run the game myself.

 

I check if the buttons look strange,

if the marble movement is unfun,

if the text is cut off,

and if a first-time player can understand the rules.

 

And then I have it modified specifically again.

 

If the AI has taken on much of the developer role,

I have acted as the planner, designer,

and QA.

 

What was important in this process

was not getting perfect code all at once.

 

It was the speed of making small modifications,

running quickly,

and checking with my eyes.

 

Godot made those iterations less tiring.

The fact that it is free is also hard for a 1 developer to ignore.

Godot is a free and open-source engine under the MIT license.

 

It can be used commercially,

and the content and rights of the game I made remain with me.

 

Reference: https://godotengine.org/license/

 

Of course, being free does not mean it is unconditionally good.

 

However, for an 1 developer who does not yet have revenue,

not having to worry about engine costs or policy changes first

was definitely a reassuring aspect.

 

That money and attention

could be spent on fixing the game even one more time.

That does not mean Godot was convenient in every way.

If I only wrote up to this point,

I might look like some kind of Godot evangelist.

 

However, when actually trying to release a mobile game,

there were certainly inconvenient parts as well.

 

Ads,

in-app purchases,

login,

and native functions for Android and iOS

were an entirely different matter from making a single game screen.

 

Matching plugin versions,

checking separately on each platform,

and repeatedly finding issues that looked fine inside the engine but differed on actual devices.

 

Compared to engines with vastly more users like Unity,

there were moments when it was difficult to find immediately applicable materials when stuck.

 

Just because AI writes code for you

did not mean these kinds of problems magically disappeared.

 

Rather, as the amount of code I did not understand rapidly increased,

QA involving direct execution and verification became even more important.

Still, if I were to make Quirky Ball again, would I choose Godot?

For this game, I think I would choose it again.

 

To be exact,

under the condition of using a single M2 MacBook Air

to quickly build and fix an 2D mobile game together with AI, then yes.

 

Conversely, for a large-scale 3D game or

a project that requires attaching a bunch of specific commercial SDKs,

I would also compare other engines first.

 

Choosing an engine is not a religion.

 

It is closer to choosing an engine that you can finish building with,

rather than choosing a famous engine.

 

For me, that answer was Godot.

And right now,

rather than the fact that I chose an engine,

actually releasing Quirky Ball is much more important.

Frequently Asked Questions

- Can you make a game with Godot without knowing how to code?

The barrier to entry has lowered a lot with the help of AI.

However, this does not mean you never have to look at code at all.

Explaining what went wrong

and the ability to run it directly and judge the results are still required.

 

- Is Godot best for vibe coding?

It varies by project.

I chose Godot because of light 2D mobile games and rapid iteration.

If the asset ecosystem or specific SDKs are more important, Unity might be more convenient.

 

- Is game development with Godot possible on an M2 MacBook Air?

2D mobile games like Quirky Ball could be worked on sufficiently.

However, this is experience based on my project.

With heavy 3D graphics and a larger build scale, the results could differ.
