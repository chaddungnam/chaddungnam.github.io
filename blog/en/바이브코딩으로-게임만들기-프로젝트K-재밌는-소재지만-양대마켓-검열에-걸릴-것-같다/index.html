Making a game with vibe coding, project K, an interesting concept but likely to fail review on both major app stores

Project K is still too embarrassing to be called a game.

 

My perceived development rate is

not even 10 percent.

 

There are many screens.

 

Dialogues appear,

there are farms,

there are mines,

and something similar to war runs.

 

However, based on the moment the player actually touches it,

there is almost no completed content.

 

While refactoring recently,

that fact began to show more nakedly.

 

I found an interesting subject, but it is dangerous from the start.

Project K becomes the leader of a fictional closed country

and is a satirical game about running a nation.

 

Everything is perfect on the propaganda broadcast,

but when you go to the field, the farms are ruined,

the mines are stopped,

and subordinates report by fixing the numbers nicely.

 

I found this gap interesting.

 

Rather than the player watching a dictator,

approving directly,

propagandizing directly,

it is a structure where you ultimately become the person running that system.

 

Looking only at the worldview,

it has the highest probability of being the most fun game I have thought of so far.

 

At the same time, it is also a subject that can easily cross the line.

 

I wrote censorship as the title, but it has not been caught yet.

To be exact,

I have never submitted Project K to Apple or Google.

 

So I cannot say it was actually rejected

or that it was censored.

 

However, there is definitely a review risk.

 

Apple App Review Guidelines restrict content that is insulting or offensive, while also stating that there may be exceptions for professional political satire and humor.

The guidelines state that professional political satire and humor may have exceptions.

 

Google Play Policy does not ban all virtual violence within games either,

but takes issue with hate speech, excessively realistic violence, exploitation of sensitive events,

and expressions that cause misunderstandings about relationships with real institutions.

 

Ultimately, just because it is satire

does not make it unconditionally safe,

and just because it is a political subject

does not mean it is unconditionally rejected.

 

The problem is that this ambiguous line

must be judged alone.

 

Therefore, currently, I am arranging it so that real country and person names are not used,

real flags and emblems are not copied,

and torture or execution is not directly shown.

 

However, if I make it too safe,

this time the satire becomes bland.

 

This tightrope walk is harder than expected.

 

However, the bigger problem right now is not market review.

Honestly, in the current state,

this is not even the stage to worry about failing reviews.

 

The completeness of the game is a much bigger problem.

 

Automated tests run.

 

Pressing the button moves to the next screen,

it saves,

and even if you quit in the middle, you can continue playing.

 

From a developer's perspective,

it looks like a game with quite a few features.

 

However, from a player's perspective,

it is just prototypes where pressing buttons changes numbers,

connected across multiple chapters.

 

The fact that tests passed

and the fact that a game is fun

had really nothing to do with each other.

 

When I refactored, all the hidden shoddy parts came out.

The reason I started refactoring was simple.

 

As scenes increased, the code tangled,

fixing one line of dialogue broke other screens,

and similar functions were duplicated across multiple files.

 

So I organized the save structure and screen flow,

began separating farms, mines, and wars

into their own respective contents.

 

The code became better than before.

 

However, once the code was cleaned up,

all the problems that had been hiding behind it became fully visible.

 

The assets had different art styles for each screen,

the lines spoke like manuals rather than characters,

and each piece of content only differed in appearance,

ultimately taking the form of pressing similar buttons.

 

Trying to save the game through refactoring,

I arrived at the conclusion that the game needed to be remade.

 

The assets have multiplied, but the face of the game has vanished.

When doing vibe coding,

the speed of creating asset candidates is truly fast.

 

The problem is not that good assets are made quickly,

but that even assets you will not use pile up quickly.

 

On some days, illustrations with heavy shading looked good,

on other days, limited-palette pixels looked good,

and on yet another day, low-poly 3D seemed like the answer.

 

Viewed individually, they are not bad.

 

However, when put into a single game,

it looks as if three different games were forcefully glued together.

 

In the current project documentation,

it is noted that screens using temporary procedural assets

should not be marked as launch-complete.

 

Call it a rule if you will,

but it practically means reviewing almost all current screens again.

 

There is plenty of dialogue, but it still lacks a human touch.

In Project K, perhaps the most important thing

is conversation rather than war or farming.

 

Subordinates manipulate statistics,

secretaries hide uncomfortable truths,

and AI nations talk about democracy

while having to control people through other means.

 

However, the current dialogue

is closer to the level of explaining the situation

and telling you to press the next button.

 

Each character has a different title,

yet their voices sound similar.

 

Their desires are weak,

what they hide from each other is insufficient,

and reading just a single sentence makes it hard to distinguish who is speaking.

 

Rather than fixing the shape of the dialogue box,

the people need to be rewritten from scratch.

 

It is not fun.

Vehicles move,

and you can recruit troops to wage war.

 

The problem is that after finishing one thing,

the motivation for why you should do the next thing is weak.

 

The food harvested from the farm

does not clearly show which people it saved,

 

and the resources gained from the mine

weakly reflect what political problems they created.

 

Looking strictly at the visuals, war seems the most game-like.

 

 

However, right now it is closer to a tech demo where you summon units

and health bars shrink.

 

At first, units even moved with the moonwalk.

 

When a war ends,

players do not know what choices they should regret,

nor why they should play this game again.

 

 

The entrance to the content has been built,

but memorable events inside it are still absent.

 

Therefore, rather than making more, we are currently tearing things down.

For the time being, attaching new features makes no sense.

 

Making another fancy globe,

adding a few more diplomatic cards,

or throwing in one more type of war unit

will not give the game a center of gravity.

 

What is being re-examined right now is the first 30 minutes.

 

Whether the very first lines are funny,

whether the first approval feels like a real choice,

whether the first field operation stays in the hands,

I am reviewing once again whether the initial reward makes people curious about the next event.

 

I am also fixing the asset direction to a single one,

rewriting the dialogue for each character,

and if each piece of content fails to prove a unique tactile feel,

I intend to boldly remove it.

 

Among the screens included in this post right now,

some might completely disappear in the next version.

 

Even so, there is a reason to keep making this game.

The subject matter of Project K is still interesting.

 

The gap between propaganda and reality,

failure packaged as numbers,

and the contradiction where you believed you made the right choice,

but as a result, it only makes the system more rigid,

is worth turning into a game.

 

However, the subject matter being interesting

and the game being fun are two different issues.

 

Right now, Project K

has grabbed an interesting subject,

but the assets, dialogue, and content

are currently looking at completely different games.

 

That is why I am taking it apart again.

 

Before the review by the two major app stores,

this is to make it a game that can first pass the review of the players.

 

It is definitely a long way to go compared to Quirky Ball, which is a simple board and puzzle game, but

as complex as it is, I want to try making a polished game and earning money from it.
